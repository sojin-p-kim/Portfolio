3D-Printed Airless Bicycle Tire

Polymer Engineering Center, UW–Madison · 2024 · Hexagonal lattice design for a puncture-proof, 3D-printed tire
SolidWorks FDM / TPU Fill Boundary Pattern Compression Testing Stratasys F370 Bambu Lab X1 Carbon

Overview

A team project to design and 3D print an airless bicycle tire that eliminates the risk of flats from punctures. Non-pneumatic tires (NPTs) remove air pressure from the equation entirely, but the challenge is replicating the ride comfort a pneumatic tire gets from air with a printable solid structure instead. Our approach: a hexagonal lattice inner structure, printed in flexible TPU, tuned by iterating on cell size and spacing.

Design

Existing airless tire designs generally fall into curved (automotive), circular (bicycle), or lattice patterns. We designed a hexagonal lattice specifically for a bicycle tire, since honeycomb geometry tessellates with no wasted material, distributes impact loads evenly, and is well known for strong energy absorption relative to its weight.

The tire matches standard 700×23c road tire dimensions — 700 mm outer diameter, 23 mm width — with the inner diameter opened up to 460 mm to fit more hexagonal cells. Each hexagon was set to a 5 mm diameter; after testing showed a 1 mm cell spacing caused excessive deformation, 2.5 mm spacing was selected as the best balance between structural integrity and print cost.

Material & manufacturing

Thermoplastic polyurethane (TPU) was selected for its rubber-like elasticity and durability — TPU 92A has a Shore hardness of 92 and 550% elongation at break, comparable to natural rubber. The lattice was modeled in SolidWorks using the Fill Boundary pattern feature, then printed via Fused Deposition Modeling (FDM) on a Stratasys F370 and later a Bambu Lab X1 Carbon after a print-head jam and filament shortage forced a printer switch mid-project.

Testing

Since the load on a bike tire is carried mainly through sidewall compression and spoke tension rather than internal air pressure, testing centered on compressive loading of printed sections. A flat rectangular section (Stratasys print) was loaded vertically to a normalized stress–strain curve, while a curved section (Bambu print) was loaded through a metal rod proxy to simulate contact-patch loading.

  • The flat section showed a linear stress–strain response up to ~6.5% strain, after which the part began to buckle sideways rather than deform structurally
  • The curved section held up to 400,000 N/m² with no permanent deformation and fully recovered within about a second of load removal — deformation stayed local to the load point rather than spreading through the structure
  • Observed strain rates were in a similar range to a typical pneumatic tire under normal riding loads

All deformation observed stayed within the elastic regime — no permanent damage occurred in either compression test, demonstrating that a 3D-printed hexagonal-lattice TPU tire can plausibly replace a pneumatic tire's shock absorption without air pressure.

Future work

  • Evaluate heat dissipation under sustained riding friction
  • Test fiber-reinforced TPU filaments to reduce buckling and localized deformation
  • Build and test a full-scale tire under real road conditions rather than isolated sections
  • Resolve print-reliability issues (head jams, filament availability) for longer full-scale prints

Skills gained

  • Lattice/lightweight structure design in SolidWorks (Fill Boundary patterning)
  • FDM printing with flexible filaments (TPU), across two different printer platforms
  • Mechanical testing: compression testing, stress–strain normalization, elastic vs. plastic deformation analysis
  • Technical writing and literature review for a multi-author engineering report
