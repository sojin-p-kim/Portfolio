Pathogen Detection via Hydrodynamic Cavitation

Dept. of Biomedical Engineering, University of Utah · Improving E. coli recovery from ground beef for electrochemical detection
Hydrodynamic Cavitation Microfluidics Immunomagnetic Separation Gold Nanoparticles Electrochemical Detection E. coli O157:H7

Overview

E. coli contamination in undercooked ground beef causes an estimated 73,000 illnesses a year in the U.S. Standard pathogen-separation methods like stomaching (mechanical agitation) plateau quickly, since bacterial detachment stalls once cell concentration hits a limit. This thesis tests whether hydrodynamic cavitation — using pressure-driven vapor bubble collapse to physically disrupt cells — can recover more E. coli from ground beef than stomaching alone, measured through a lab-built electrochemical detection pipeline.

How hydrodynamic cavitation works

A custom cavitation chamber was built by laser-cutting five stacked acrylic disks: four wide "expansion chamber" disks with 1.2 mm holes sandwiching one constricted "orifice plate" disk with 0.8 mm holes. As fluid moves from the wide chambers into the narrow orifice, velocity increases and static pressure drops, forming vapor bubbles. As the fluid exits back into a wide chamber, pressure rises again and the bubbles collapse — releasing energy that disrupts bacterial cells.

The lab-built cavitation chamber (left) vs. a standard stomacher (right) — the two separation methods compared in this study

Detection pipeline

After separation, E. coli was pulled out and detected in three stages:

  • Immunomagnetic capture: streptavidin-coated magnetic polystyrene beads, coated with anti-E. coli antibodies, bind and trap E. coli from the sample
  • Gold nanoparticle labeling: anti-E. coli-coated gold nanoparticles bind the captured bacteria; when exposed to HCl, the gold gives up electrons more readily, generating a measurable current
  • Electrochemical detection: current response is measured with CHI electrodes on a magnetic concentration platform — a larger (more negative) current signal indicates greater bacterial recovery

Results

Ground beef samples inoculated with E. coli were processed three ways — no treatment (reference), stomaching alone, and stomaching plus hydrodynamic cavitation — then measured for electrochemical current response.

Stomaching alone barely moved the needle over the untreated reference (3% recovery increase). Adding hydrodynamic cavitation on top of stomaching produced a clearly stronger signal — a 33% increase in recovery over baseline — showing that cavitation meaningfully improves pathogen recovery beyond what mechanical agitation achieves on its own.

Limitations & future work

  • The electrochemical signal can't distinguish E. coli from other bacterial pathogens the antibodies might cross-react with, so a very clean experimental environment is required
  • The full protocol takes over 3 hours, which limits practicality for rapid food-safety screening as-is
  • Next steps include applying the method to hospital/surgical settings for faster pathogen diagnosis, and further optimizing cavitation parameters to shorten processing time
